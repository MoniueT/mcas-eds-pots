hEDS and HSD: symptoms, the Beighton score and what to track

Hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and hypermobility spectrum disorder can mean unstable joints, pain and fatigue that change from day to day. Here's what they are and what's worth keeping a record of.

What are hEDS and HSD?

Ehlers-Danlos syndromes (EDS) are a group of inherited conditions that affect connective tissue, the material that supports your skin, joints, blood vessels and organs. There are 13 types. The most common by far is hypermobile EDS (hEDS).

Most types of EDS can be confirmed with a genetic test, but hEDS can't yet. It's diagnosed by a doctor using a set of clinical criteria published in 2017. People who have symptoms caused by hypermobile joints but don't meet all of the hEDS criteria may be diagnosed with hypermobility spectrum disorder (HSD). HSD can be just as troublesome as hEDS, and the day to day management is usually much the same.

Common symptoms

  • Joints that bend further than usual
  • Joints that slip partly out of place (subluxations) or fully out (dislocations)
  • Long-term joint and muscle pain
  • Fatigue
  • Soft or stretchy skin, easy bruising or slow healing
  • Gut problems such as reflux, bloating and constipation
  • Dizziness and a fast heartbeat on standing, which can be linked to POTS

The Beighton score

The Beighton score is a quick 9-point check of how flexible some of your joints are. It looks at your little fingers, thumbs, elbows, knees and whether you can put your palms flat on the floor with straight legs. It's one part of how doctors assess hypermobility. A high score on its own doesn't mean you have hEDS, and a lower score doesn't rule it out, especially as people often become less flexible with age.

How hEDS and HSD are usually managed

There's no cure, so treatment focuses on managing symptoms and protecting joints. Common approaches include:

  • Physiotherapy to strengthen the muscles around joints and improve body awareness
  • Pacing, which means spreading activity out and planning rest to avoid boom and bust
  • Bracing or supports for higher risk activities
  • Pain management, agreed with your doctor
  • Help with linked conditions such as POTS, MCAS and gut problems

What to track if you have EDS or HSD

  • Joint events: which joint, which side, a subluxation or dislocation, and whether it went back on its own
  • Pain and fatigue, rated the same way each day so changes are easy to see
  • Activity and rest, because overdoing it often shows up as pain or a joint event the following day
  • PT, stretches and brace days, to see what helps over weeks rather than days
  • Dizziness and gut symptoms, which may point to POTS or MCAS as well

Over a few months, a record like this can show which joints are most unstable, whether physio is making a difference, and how often payback follows a busy day.

Common questions

What's the difference between hEDS and HSD?

Both involve symptomatic joint hypermobility. hEDS is diagnosed when someone meets all of the 2017 criteria. HSD is used when someone has symptoms from hypermobile joints but doesn't meet every criterion. HSD isn't a milder condition, and it's managed in much the same way.

Why does EDS often come with POTS and MCAS?

Researchers don't fully know yet. Stretchier connective tissue in blood vessels may let blood pool in the legs, which could contribute to POTS. Links with MCAS are still being studied. Read more about the overlap.
