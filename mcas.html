MCAS: symptoms, triggers and how to track them

Mast cell activation syndrome can affect your skin, gut, breathing, heart and head, often all at once. Here's a plain English guide to what it is, what can set it off, and what's worth keeping track of.

What is MCAS?

Mast cells are part of your immune system. They sit in your skin, gut, airways and around blood vessels, and when they sense a threat they release chemicals such as histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins and leukotrienes. That's useful against infections and allergens.

In mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS), mast cells release these chemicals too easily or too often, in response to things that shouldn't cause a reaction. Because mast cells are found all over the body, the symptoms can show up in several body systems at once, and they often come and go in episodes.

MCAS is different from mastocytosis, where there are too many mast cells. In MCAS the number of mast cells is usually normal, but they're overreactive.

Common MCAS symptoms

Symptoms vary a lot from person to person. They commonly include:

Body systemSymptoms people often report
SkinFlushing, itching, hives, rashes, burning or swelling
GutNausea, stomach cramps, bloating, diarrhoea, reflux
AirwaysBlocked or runny nose, wheezing, throat tightness
Heart and circulationFast heartbeat, dizziness, feeling faint, low blood pressure
Brain and nervesBrain fog, headaches, fatigue, trouble concentrating

Severe reactions with throat swelling, difficulty breathing or collapse need emergency help straight away. Call 999 and use an adrenaline pen if you've been prescribed one.

Common MCAS triggers

Triggers are personal, which is why tracking matters so much. Ones that come up often include:

  • Heat, sudden temperature changes and hot showers
  • Exercise or physical exertion
  • Stress, poor sleep and illness
  • Friction or pressure on the skin
  • Alcohol
  • Foods high in histamine, such as aged cheese, cured meats, fermented foods and leftovers
  • Strong smells, perfumes and cleaning products
  • Some medicines, such as NSAIDs, and some anaesthetics
  • Hormonal changes, including around periods

How MCAS is usually diagnosed

There isn't one single test. Specialists usually look for three things together:

  • Episodes of symptoms affecting two or more body systems
  • Lab evidence of mast cell chemicals rising during a reaction. A common one is a blood tryptase test taken within a few hours of a reaction, compared with a baseline taken on a good day
  • Symptoms improving with treatments that target mast cells, such as antihistamines or mast cell stabilisers

Because the tests depend on timing, a clear record of when reactions happen, how long they last and what came before them can make a real difference.

What to track if you have MCAS

  • Symptoms by body system, rated by severity, so you can show reactions affect more than one system
  • Food, including leftovers and high-histamine foods, because reactions can come hours later
  • Everyday triggers such as heat, stress, smells, exercise and poor sleep
  • What helped, such as extra antihistamines, rest or a cool shower, and how long the episode lasted
  • Medicines and dose changes, so you and your doctor can judge whether a treatment is working
  • Blood results, especially tryptase, with the date and whether it was during a reaction or a baseline

Mast cell reactions often build up over a day or two, so a single trigger on its own may not cause a problem but several together can. Looking back across 48 hours is often more useful than looking at the day of the reaction alone.

MCAS, EDS and POTS

MCAS is often reported alongside hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome or hypermobility spectrum disorder and POTS. Mast cell chemicals can widen blood vessels and speed up the heart, which can make POTS symptoms worse. Read more about how the three connect.

Common questions

Is MCAS the same as an allergy?

Not quite. In an allergy, the immune system reacts to a specific trigger such as pollen or peanuts through an antibody called IgE. In MCAS, mast cells release their chemicals more easily in general, often to a mix of triggers, and allergy tests can be normal.

Why do my MCAS symptoms seem to come out of nowhere?

Often several smaller triggers add up, such as a warm room, a stressful day and a high-histamine meal. People sometimes call this the bucket effect. Tracking across a couple of days can show which combinations tend to tip you over.

Can a low-histamine diet help?

Some people find it helps calm symptoms for a while, but it's usually meant as a short phase followed by careful reintroduction, ideally with support from a dietitian or doctor, so your diet doesn't become more limited than it needs to be.
