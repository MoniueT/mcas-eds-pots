POTS: symptoms, the stand test and what to track

POTS makes standing up hard work for your body. Here's what's going on, how it's usually diagnosed and managed, and what's worth keeping a record of.

What is POTS?

Postural tachycardia syndrome (POTS, sometimes written PoTS) is a condition where your heart rate goes up a lot when you stand up, along with symptoms such as dizziness, a racing heart and fatigue. It's a type of dysautonomia, which means the part of the nervous system that controls things like heart rate and blood pressure isn't working as it should.

When most people stand, their body quickly tightens blood vessels to keep blood flowing to the brain. In POTS this doesn't work well, so the heart speeds up to make up for it.

Common POTS symptoms

  • Dizziness or lightheadedness when standing
  • A fast, pounding or fluttering heartbeat
  • Fatigue, often worse after standing for a while
  • Brain fog and trouble concentrating
  • Feeling faint, or fainting
  • Shakiness, sweating and feeling too hot or too cold
  • Headaches, nausea and poor sleep
  • Legs turning purple or blotchy when standing, from blood pooling

Symptoms are often worse in the heat, after a big meal, after alcohol, during illness, around periods and in the morning.

How POTS is usually diagnosed

POTS is usually diagnosed when your heart rate rises by at least 30 beats per minute within 10 minutes of standing (40 or more in teenagers), without a big drop in blood pressure, and you've had symptoms for several months. Doctors check this with an active stand test, where you lie down and then stand while your heart rate is measured, or a tilt table test. They'll usually also check for other causes, such as dehydration, anaemia or thyroid problems.

A home stand test (sometimes called a lean test) can give you useful readings to share with your doctor, but it isn't a diagnosis. Only do one if you feel well enough, have someone nearby if you're prone to fainting, and stop and sit or lie down if you feel faint.

How POTS is usually managed

Most of what helps is day to day. Common advice includes:

  • Drinking plenty of fluids through the day
  • Extra salt, but only if your doctor says it's right for you
  • Compression tights, leggings or abdominal binders
  • Getting up slowly, and avoiding standing still for long periods
  • Smaller, more frequent meals
  • Exercise that starts lying down or seated, such as rowing, recumbent cycling or swimming, building up slowly
  • Medicines, for some people, prescribed by a specialist

What to track if you have POTS

  • Heart rate lying and standing, taken the same way each time, so you can see the rise
  • Symptoms such as dizziness, palpitations, fatigue and brain fog
  • Fluids and salt, against the targets you've agreed with your doctor
  • Compression days
  • Triggers such as heat, standing still, big meals, alcohol, poor sleep and illness
  • Medicines and dose changes, with the date, so you can judge what's working

Repeating a home stand test every week or two, at a similar time of day, builds a much clearer picture than a single reading.

Common questions

Is POTS the same as low blood pressure?

No. In POTS the heart rate rises a lot on standing, but blood pressure usually stays fairly steady. When blood pressure drops significantly on standing, that's called orthostatic hypotension, which is a different condition.

How do I do a lean test at home?

Usually you lie down for about 5 minutes and take your heart rate, then stand still, leaning your shoulders against a wall, and take readings at 2, 5 and 10 minutes. Always check with your doctor first, have someone nearby, and stop if you feel faint. TrinityMate has a guided version with a timer that beeps when each reading is due.

Why are my POTS symptoms worse in the heat?

Heat widens blood vessels, which lets more blood pool in the legs and makes it harder for the body to keep blood flowing to the brain. Keeping cool, drinking more and avoiding long hot showers often helps.
